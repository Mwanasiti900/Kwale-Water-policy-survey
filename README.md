## 📋 Executive Summary

Access to clean, reliable water remains one of the defining socio-economic constraints for households in Kwale County, a predominantly rural, coastal county in Kenya. This project evaluates the **efficiency of an ongoing water access and social protection policy**, examining whether resource allocation, infrastructure delivery, and governance mechanisms are producing equitable, measurable improvements in household welfare, health outcomes, and economic productivity.

Against a policy target of **75% household water access**, the county currently sits at **61%**, with performance ranging from **71% in Matuga to 49% in Lunga Lunga** a 22 point gap that signals uneven implementation rather than a uniform resourcing shortfall. Households in the lowest income quintile spend **nearly 4× the share of income** on water compared to the wealthiest quintile, and **34% of households** report a waterborne illness in the past year, rising sharply in dry-season months.

Using a mixed methods approach combining quantitative KPI tracking with qualitative thematic analysis of community and stakeholder input this analysis moves beyond "was water delivered?" to the more consequential policy question: **"did it change lives, and for whom?"**

> 💡 **Why this matters:** Policy efficiency is not just a delivery metric it is a distributional one. A policy can meet its infrastructure targets and still fail the households it was designed to protect.

## 🔍 Root Cause Analysis — Why the Problem Persists

The persistence of water insecurity in Kwale is not attributable to a single failure point, but to the interaction of structural, institutional, and environmental factors:

- **Infrastructure deficits** — aging or absent piped water networks in rural and peri urban wards
- **Fragmented financing** — inconsistent budget disbursement between national and county level water authorities
- **Implementation gaps** — divergence between policy design (on paper) and on the ground delivery
- **Climate variability** — seasonal rainfall inconsistency affecting borehole and surface water reliability
- **Data and monitoring gaps** — limited feedback loops between community experience and policymaking bodies
- **Governance and accountability** — weak mechanisms for community participation in water resource decision making

> 🧩 This analysis treats these as **interdependent variables**, not isolated causes a policy fix at one layer often stalls without corresponding movement at another.

## 🧭 Methodology

**Phase 1 — Data Cleaning & Validation**
Consolidation of household survey data, county water authority records, and qualitative transcripts. Applied consistency checks, deduplication, and outlier validation to ensure analytical integrity before KPI construction.

**Phase 2 — KPI Framework Design**
Developed a policy-aligned KPI set spanning **access, equity, economic burden, and health outcomes** designed to be interpretable by both technical and non-technical policy stakeholders.

**Phase 3 — Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)**
Identified distribution patterns, ward-level disparities, and time series trends in water access and household expenditure using SQL and Excel.

**Phase 4 — Qualitative Thematic Analysis**
Coded interview and consultation data into recurring themes trust in local governance, perceived fairness of resource distribution, and coping mechanisms to contextualize the quantitative findings.

**Phase 5 — Visualisation & Dashboard Development**
Translated validated KPIs into an interactive Power BI dashboard for policy stakeholders, prioritising clarity over decorative complexity.

**Phase 6 — Policy Interpretation & Impact Assessment**
Synthesized quantitative and qualitative findings into a coherent efficiency assessment, benchmarked against stated policy objectives.

> 📌 **Methodological rigor:** Quantitative KPIs were consistently triangulated against qualitative narratives to avoid the common analytical trap of mistaking *output delivery* for *outcome achievement*.

## 🔑 Key Findings

1. **Delivery is off-track against the policy target.** County-wide access sits at 61% against a 75% target, with the gap widening rather than closing at the current rate of improvement (+2–4 points per year).
2. **The policy benefit is not reaching the most water-insecure wards.** Lunga Lunga and Kinango the two lowest-performing wards sit 12–22 points below the county average, while Matuga significantly outperforms the target baseline, pointing to uneven implementation rather than a uniform resource shortfall.
3. **The economic burden of water access is regressive.** The lowest income quintile spends nearly 4× the income share on water compared to the highest quintile, meaning the households least able to absorb the cost carry the heaviest burden.
4. **Health and education outcomes move with water access.** Waterborne illness incidence peaks at 41% in the dry season (Q3), coinciding with increased reliance on unprotected sources, and 21% of adolescent girls report missing school for water-related duties.
5. **Qualitative interviews reveal a governance trust gap.** Community consultations indicate that perceived fairness in how new water points are sited is a stronger predictor of local buy-in than the number of water points delivered.

> 🎯 **Critical insight:** The policy's core inefficiency is **distributional, not developmental** the intervention is under targeting the wards and income groups with the greatest need, which limits socio-economic impact even where infrastructure spending is on budget.

## 🧠 Policy Interpretation

This section evaluates findings against the policy's stated intent, assessing:

- **Alignment with SDG 6** (Clean Water and Sanitation): the county is progressing but off-pace to meet 2030 targets at the current rate of improvement.
- **Consistency with Kwale County Integrated Development Plan (CIDP)** priorities: infrastructure investment is occurring, but ward-level sequencing does not consistently prioritize the lowest-access areas identified in this analysis.
- **Distributional fairness**: policy benefits are concentrating in better served wards (Matuga, Samburu), while the wards with the greatest need (Lunga Lunga, Kinango) continue to lag a pattern consistent with elite capture or infrastructure readiness bias in project siting.
- **Implementation efficiency**: the gap between budgeted intervention and realized household-level impact suggests delivery bottlenecks between disbursement and last-mile completion, rather than a lack of overall funding.

> ⚖️ A policy can be operationally "successful" by delivery metrics while remaining socio-economically inefficient if the benefit distribution does not match need.

## ✅ Recommendations

| Recommendation | Rationale | Expected Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-weight infrastructure investment toward Lunga Lunga and Kinango** using a needs based allocation formula rather than infrastructure-readiness criteria | These wards show the lowest access rates (49% and 55%) and are furthest from the county average, indicating unmet need rather than delivery saturation | Narrows the equity distribution score and reduces the 22-point ward level gap within 2–3 budget cycles |
| **Introduce a tiered, income-sensitive cost-recovery model** for community water points | Lowest-income households currently pay a disproportionate income share (15% vs. 4% for the highest quintile) | Reduces the regressive burden and improves affordability without reducing total cost recovery |
| **Establish a pre-dry-season maintenance and water-quality surge protocol** | Illness incidence peaks 11 points above baseline in Q3, aligned with increased reliance on unprotected sources | Reduces seasonal spikes in waterborne illness and associated healthcare costs |
| **Formalize a community feedback and siting-transparency mechanism** | Qualitative data shows perceived fairness in site selection outweighs sheer infrastructure volume in shaping community trust | Improves governance legitimacy and long-term adoption of new water points |
